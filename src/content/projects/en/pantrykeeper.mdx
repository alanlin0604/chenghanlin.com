Shared tracking of household stock and expiry dates. The point is not that it
can record things — a spreadsheet does that — but that it gets **several people
to keep maintaining the same list.**

## The problem

A fridge and a pantry are shared, but tracking tools assume one user. So one
person builds the list, nobody else updates it, and within a fortnight the data
has drifted from reality. After that nobody opens it at all.

The second problem is **the cost of the first entry.** Ask someone to type in
forty items by hand and most will stop around the fifth.

## Technical points

**Per-member permissions.** Household members are not interchangeable — one
person does the shopping, another only needs to look things up. Each member can
have a different scope of action, so sharing does not have to mean everyone can
change everything.

**Offline support.** The real usage context is standing in front of a fridge or
between supermarket shelves, and reception in both is usually poor. Working
offline is not a nice extra here; it decides whether the product gets used at
all.

**A bulk import parser.** This is what removes the start-up cost: paste
straight from Notion, Excel or Google Sheets and the parser infers the item,
the quantity and the unit from free-form text.

The difficulty is that **pasted data has no fixed shape.** "Milk 2 bottles",
"eggs x10" and "flour 1kg" are three ways of writing the same kind of
information. The parser has to tolerate that rather than demand the data be
tidied first — because if tidying is required, typing it in by hand was never
the slower option.

**Waste statistics.** Recording what expired and got thrown out shows people
what they are actually wasting. This is the part that turns tracking into
changed behaviour: knowing what is in the fridge does not alter how someone
shops; knowing that three bags of vegetables were binned last month does.
